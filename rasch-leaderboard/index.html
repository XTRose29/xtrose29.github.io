WORKING PROPOSAL · OCTOBER 2026

A leaderboard
that learns.

Estimate solver ability and problem difficulty together. Then choose evaluations that balance useful proofs, low cost, and better predictions.

Tianruo Rose XuRasch model + adaptive samplingDraft for discussion

THE CENTRAL IDEA

The gap matters.

A stronger solver on a harder problem can have the same success probability as a weaker solver on an easier problem.

P(solve) = 1 / (1 + exp(−(ability − difficulty)))

Rasch / one-parameter logistic model. The controls below illustrate the equation; they are not measured results. Model reference ↗

ILLUSTRATIVE PREDICTION73%

Increase both by the same amount: the prediction stays the same.

01

Learn two sets of scores

Give each solver an ability parameter and each proof node a difficulty parameter. Fit a regularized Rasch model to verified success/failure outcomes, updating after each batch. Start with neutral priors, or use initial difficulty estimates as weak priors.

Fix mean node difficulty at zero to anchor the scale. Use Gaussian priors to keep sparse or all-success/all-failure estimates finite. Show uncertainty alongside ratings; an Elo-style number can be a display scale, not a separate model.

02

Keep speed and cost separate

Reuse estimated node difficulty as a candidate predictor for two secondary models: runtime and dollars per attempt. Each solver gets separate time and cost parameters; its proof ability does not determine its efficiency.

Fit to all attempts, including failures. Test whether shared difficulty improves held-out predictions over solver-only baselines. It is a hypothesis to validate, not an assumption to enforce.

03

Start small, with shared anchors

A proposed pilot: 3 solvers × 12 nodes × 2 repeats = 72 attempts, subject to an agreed dollar cap. Include easy, medium, and hard nodes, and give every solver the same initial nodes so their scores are comparable.

Pin solver versions, Lean checks, context, and attempt limits. Use fresh runs; record outcome, actual cost, time, and selection probability. Budget exhaustion counts as failure to solve within the limit; infrastructure errors are tracked separately.

04

Mix progress with learning

After the pilot, use this simple starting allocation of attempt slots. These percentages are tunable choices, not claimed optimal settings.

60%30%10%
60% · Solve efficiently
On unsolved nodes, favor high predicted success per expected dollar, within a latency limit.
30% · Reduce uncertainty
Favor uncertain solver–node gaps with success probabilities near 50%, adjusted for expected cost. Use p(1−p) × Var(ability − difficulty) / cost as an initial heuristic.
10% · Explore broadly
Randomize across eligible pairs to preserve coverage, revisit uncertain rankings, and connect new solvers or nodes to the anchor set.

Enforce a hard total budget across all three groups. Keep a separate, uniformly sampled evaluation set for calibration; adaptively selected raw success rates alone are not a fair ranking.

05

Know when to pause—or add harder nodes

Track two signals separately: estimate stability (uncertainty and pairwise rankings change little) and benchmark saturation (strong solvers solve almost everything).

As a pilot rule, flag saturation when a leading solver exceeds 95% closure on the fixed evaluation set, with enough repeats and an uncertainty interval. A high point estimate alone is insufficient. Keep anchor nodes when adding harder ones, version the pool, and refit; do not compare unanchored scores across pools.

06

Evaluate the evaluation strategy

Compare the mixed policy with uniform sampling under the same dollar budget. Measure verified nodes solved, held-out probability calibration, ranking uncertainty, total spend, and elapsed time. Report solver pairs by the posterior probability that one ability exceeds the other.

First test the loop on simulated outcomes, then run the small real pilot. Adjust the sampling mixture based on those results.

QUESTIONS FOR DISCUSSION

What should the next dollar buy?

Should the first pilot prioritize more solved nodes or more reliable ability estimates? Is the 60/30/10 split a reasonable baseline? Does one shared difficulty axis explain enough of the variation in cost and runtime?
